# NW_STD_0417: Gearbox vibration monitoring and alarm limits

Northwind Energy engineering standard | Rev. 4 | approved by Priya Raman | effective 1 April 2026

## Scope and normative references

This standard defines how Northwind Energy monitors gearbox vibration on its offshore fleet in the Danish North Sea, and it sets the levels at which action is required. It applies to every geared turbine at the Esbjerg Nord and Horns Vest sites, whether supplied by Halvard Drivetrain or Brantvik Gear Systems, and it applies to both permanently installed condition monitoring and to handheld route measurements. It does not cover direct drive machines, which have no gearbox and are governed separately, and it does not cover generator or blade bearing monitoring. The velocity levels here are expressed as broadband RMS in millimetres per second, measured over the band defined in the measurement section below, and they are consistent with the general severity guidance for this machine class in ISO 10816. Where this standard is stricter than the turbine supplier's own published limits, this standard governs, because the supplier limits are written for warranty purposes and this standard is written to catch damage early enough to plan a vessel. This revision is Rev. 4, approved by Priya Raman, lead reliability engineer.

## Why a single overall level is not sufficient

An overall broadband level is a single number summarising all the vibration energy in the measured band, and it is genuinely useful because it is cheap, stable and trendable. It is also, on its own, a poor detector of the most common serious gearbox fault, and the reason is arithmetic rather than opinion. Bearing raceway damage in its early stage produces short, low energy impulses. Spread across a wide measurement band and combined in quadrature with the much larger gear mesh and shaft components that are always present, those impulses contribute very little to the total. A raceway can carry a through crack and a growing pit while the overall level moves by a few tenths of a millimetre per second, which is within the range that normal load and temperature variation produces anyway. By the time the damage is extensive enough to lift the overall level clearly, the failure is in its self accelerating phase and the remaining useful life is measured in days. Setting the overall alarm lower does not solve this: it produces false alarms from benign operating variation without materially improving early detection. The answer is not a lower threshold on the same number. It is a second criterion that looks at the shape of the spectrum instead of its total.

## The sideband criterion

Early raceway damage announces itself by modulation rather than by magnitude. Each pass of a rolling element over a damaged zone produces an impulse, those impulses repeat at the bearing pass frequency determined by geometry and shaft speed, and they amplitude modulate the strong gear mesh vibration that is always present. Amplitude modulation in the frequency domain does not appear as a peak at the modulating frequency, which is the point most often missed: it appears as pairs of sidebands straddling the carrier, offset above and below it by the modulating frequency. So the diagnostic pattern is symmetric sidebands around the gear mesh frequency, spaced at the calculated bearing pass frequency, typically with first, second and third order pairs visible and amplitude falling as order rises. This pattern is specific: normal gear wear raises the mesh peak and its harmonics without producing pass frequency sidebands, and looseness or misalignment produces shaft order families instead. Because the pattern is specific and appears early, it is the primary criterion in this standard. The presence of a clear pass frequency sideband family requires spectral review and a shortened inspection interval regardless of the overall level, and it does so on its own authority: a nominal overall reading is not grounds to dismiss it.

## Levels and why they sit where they do

Three levels apply to the broadband velocity at the high speed stage. Below 3.2 mm/s RMS the unit is nominal and the normal route interval applies. At or above 3.2 mm/s RMS the unit is at warning: the spectrum is reviewed by the condition monitoring analyst, currently Mette Sorensen, the trend rate is assessed, and the inspection interval is shortened. At or above 4.5 mm/s RMS the unit is in alarm: the turbine is stopped and the drivetrain is inspected before it returns to service. The alarm level is not derived from a severity chart alone. It is set where the expected remaining life at the observed progression rate for this machine class still leaves time to mobilise a vessel and a crane, because a limit that is exceeded only when repair is already impossible to plan has no operational value. That reasoning is also why the alarm sits above the warning level by a deliberately narrow margin: the interval between them is intended to be short in time, and the warning is where the useful work happens. Treating the alarm as the real threshold and the warning as advisory inverts the intent of this standard.

## Measurement method and record keeping

Measurements are taken at the high speed stage bearing housing in the radial direction, at the permanently marked location, with the machine at or above seventy percent of rated power and with the drivetrain at operating temperature. Readings taken below that load or on a cold drivetrain are not comparable to the trend and are recorded as indicative only. The broadband figure is the RMS velocity from 10 Hz to 1000 Hz. Spectral review additionally requires a resolution fine enough to separate sidebands spaced at the calculated bearing pass frequency from the gear mesh peak, which in practice means a line spacing several times finer than that spacing; a spectrum too coarse to resolve the sidebands cannot be used to dismiss the sideband criterion, and reviewing one is a recorded non conformance. Every measurement is recorded against the asset tag of the gearbox, not the turbine, so that a unit which moves between positions keeps its own history and a replacement unit does not inherit the history of the one it replaced. Where a reading leads to an intervention, the work order reference is recorded alongside it, and where a strip down establishes a mechanism, the root cause report reference is recorded as well.
